## Key Findings

- Overall sunk-cost effect is moderate: Cohen's d (ES) = 0.496 across k = 100 effect sizes (95% CI 0.364 to 0.628;
  fail-safe N = 148), with very high heterogeneity (Q = 1,337.2, df 99). Utilization decisions d = 0.581 (k = 38);
  progress decisions (continue funding a project) d = 0.443 (k = 62); the two do not differ significantly (Table 2).
- Progress decisions are the relevant ones for abandoning a failing project. There, the effect does NOT fade with
  time: a high delay between the initial and later decision raises it (b = 0.421, p < 0.05), unlike utilization
  decisions where time attenuates it. The authors read elapsed time as itself an investment in the project.
- Knowing the theory does not protect: familiarity with economic decision-making had no significant effect (progress
  b = -0.162, n.s.; utilization b = 0.04, n.s.). Younger adults and students show larger effects in utilization
  decisions.
- Suggested mitigations are structural: barriers that force external justification, or that distribute responsibility
  across several decision-makers, plus opportunity-cost information (the latter from cited work, not tested here).
- Scope limits: only monetary sunk costs; studies on time or behavioural investment without an hourly rate were
  excluded; most data are hypothetical scenarios (field studies are few); some field experiments found no effect.
  Applying this to a solo developer's invested time is an extrapolation the paper does not make.

## Inclusion Decision

**Decision:** Supporting
**Rationale:** Level 1 evidence for the existence and size of the sunk-cost effect, and the only quantitative support
for "awareness is not enough; add structure". Supporting, not Core, because it is monetary-only and mostly
hypothetical-scenario data, and Sleesman et al. (escalation of commitment in projects) is the closer fit.

**Redundancy check:** Adds a sunk-cost-specific effect size (d about 0.44 for progress decisions) and the finding
that time and education do not reduce it. Partly overlaps t2-sleesman-escalation-meta-analysis, which measures
escalation of commitment broadly.
