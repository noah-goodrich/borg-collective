## Key Findings

- Defines an AAR as a professional discussion focused on performance standards, where participants discover for
  themselves what happened, why, and how to sustain strengths and fix weaknesses.
- Explicitly "not a critique": rank and personality are not supposed to determine who has the answers.
- Has a whole chapter on "Following Up": the value is stated to come from applying results to future work (retraining),
  i.e. the loop-closing step is part of the doctrine, not an add-on.
- Source of the widely quoted four-question structure; this card quotes only definition and follow-up text verified in
  the fetched copy.

## Verified Quote(s)

**Location reference:** Printed page numbers of TC 25-20. Quote 1: Chapter 1 "The After-Action Review", section
"Definition and Purpose of After-Action Reviews", first paragraph, p. 1. Quote 2: Chapter 4 "Conducting the
After-Action Review", section "Introduction and Rules", second bullet of the list of introductory thoughts, p. 16.
Quote 3: Chapter 5 "Following Up", section "Benefits", first sentence, p. 22.

> An after-action review (AAR) is a professional discussion of an event, focused on performance standards, that enables soldiers to discover for themselves what happened, why it happened, and how to sustain strengths and improve on weaknesses.

> An AAR is not a critique.

> The real benefits of AARs come from taking the results and applying them to future training.
