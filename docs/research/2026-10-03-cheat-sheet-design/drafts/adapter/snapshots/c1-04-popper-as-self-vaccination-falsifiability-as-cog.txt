What this paper argues: all propaganda works by the same structural mechanism — self-sealing logic that absorbs potential falsification. Karl Popper called this mechanism "immunizing stratagems" and identified it as the mark of bad reasoning. Inoculation theory (McGuire, 1961) identified psychological pre-exposure as the mechanism for building resistance to manipulation. Neither literature has connected these two observations. This paper makes the connection: teaching the falsifiability criterion is not teaching one tool against one type of manipulation. It is teaching the structural property that makes all manipulation recognizable simultaneously — regardless of its surface technique, platform, or domain. This is self-vaccination. Existing inoculation approaches face a whack-a-mole problem: manipulation techniques evolve faster than inoculation content can be produced, tested, and distributed. Technique-based prebunking requires cataloging specific techniques; fact-based inoculation requires cataloging specific false claims; logic-based inoculation requires cataloging classical fallacies. All of these approaches require ongoing updating because they are tied to specific content instances rather than the structural property common to all manipulation. The Popperian self-vaccination approach is durable because the structural property it targets — self-sealing — is stable across all manipulation techniques, all platforms, and all domains. It is general because all major propaganda techniques (conspiracy theories, whataboutism, moving the goalposts, false experts, epistemic populism) are self-sealing by construction. And it is efficient because it requires learning one criterion rather than a catalog. The paper identifies why the Popper-inoculation connection was not made historically (different academic traditions, no citation overlap), demonstrates that Popper's "immunizing stratagems" concept and McGuire's inoculation theory were independently diagnosing the same pathogen, and proposes a second-order application — the Principle of Epistemic Symmetry — under which the Popperian criterion is applied not only to claims encountered but to critiques of those claims, detecting manipulation by trusted in-group sources as well as hostile out-group sources. Empirical predictions distinguishing the self-vaccination approach from existing inoculation designs are proposed.
