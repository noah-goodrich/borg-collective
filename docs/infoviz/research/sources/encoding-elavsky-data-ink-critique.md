## Key Findings

- The data-ink ratio, taken literally to its logical extreme (via an "ink golf" exercise reducing
  a chart to near-zero ink), produces charts that are imperceptible or inaccessible — showing the
  ratio alone cannot be the design criterion; some unspecified, undefined "reasonableness"
  constraint is doing the real work.
- Tufte's original data-ink ratio guidance is, in Elavsky's words, "a rough heuristic and not even
  remotely a real, working 'theory'" — a direct challenge to treating it as a rigorous design law
  rather than a rule of thumb.
- Minimalism pushed too far becomes an accessibility barrier, not a virtue — raising the question
  of when stripping ink starts excluding readers who rely on redundant visual cues.
- Corroborating empirical evidence (Bateman et al., CHI 2010): in a controlled comparison,
  participants' accuracy describing embellished ("chartjunk"-laden) charts was no worse than for
  minimalist charts, and their recall 2–3 weeks later was significantly BETTER for the embellished
  versions — directly contradicting the assumption that decoration always harms comprehension and
  retention.
- This finding has been reaffirmed by later academic work (2013/2015 follow-up experiments,
  per search results) that re-asserts chartjunk is "not always harmful," making the challenge to
  Tufte-style minimalism a sustained, multi-study line of dissent rather than one contrarian
  blog post.
