## Key Findings

- Observers instructed to perform an attention-demanding task (counting basketball passes) frequently fail to
  notice a fully visible, unexpected event occurring in the same visual field — "out of all 192 observers
  across all conditions, 54% noticed the unexpected event," meaning a substantial fraction were completely
  inattentionally blind to a conspicuous stimulus.
  
- Noticing is not simply a function of visibility: "More observers noticed the unexpected event in the Easy
  (64%) than in the Hard" condition — the harder the primary (attention-demanding) task, the less likely
  observers were to perceive a fully salient secondary stimulus, directly supporting attention (not raw visual
  salience) as the gate to perception.
  
- Similarity between the unexpected object and the attended objects matters: detection likelihood depends on
  how visually similar the unattended, unexpected item is to the actively monitored items — a directly relevant
  design implication for dashboards where an anomaly must visually "pop" against the attended data series to be
  reliably noticed.
  
- Spatial proximity of the unattended critical object to attended locations did *not* meaingfully affect
  detection, suggesting "observers attend to objects and events, not spatial positions" — a finding that
  complicates naive assumptions that placing an alert "near" the thing people are already looking at is
  sufficient to guarantee it gets seen.
  
- The paper frames this as one instance of a broader family of "blindness" phenomena (inattentional blindness
  and change blindness) that all point to the same underlying limit: "we perceive and remember only those
  objects and details that receive focused attention" — a foundational constraint on how much complex
  operational information a viewer can be expected to register from a single glance at a dense display.

## Inclusion Decision

**Decision:** Core
**Rationale:** This is the canonical, most-cited empirical demonstration of attention-gated perception, directly
answering the "what does the eye actually register" half of RQ1/RQ4 that pure encoding-ranking papers
(Cleveland & McGill, Heer & Bostock) do not address — those papers assume the viewer is already looking at the
right place; Simons & Chabris shows that assumption is often false.

**Redundancy check:** Not redundant with the encoding-ranking papers in this set — it covers a distinct
mechanism (attentional gating of perception, not accuracy of decoding an attended encoding) that is equally
foundational to the same overall research question.
