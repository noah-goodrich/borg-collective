## Key Findings

- Bertin's 1967 *Semiology of Graphics* is the first systematic attempt at "a unified theoretical
  structure for the field of information visualization," built through iterative practical
  experimentation rather than lab studies.
- The framework identifies two planar variables (the x/y spatial position) and six "retinal"
  variables that can encode data on top of position: size, value (lightness), texture/pattern,
  color (hue), orientation, and shape.
- Bertin argued relative size is the single most broadly useful and easily perceived visual
  variable regardless of what is being visualized (per the earlier search-result synthesis,
  corroborated independently of the Medium piece).
- Its influence is deep but its readership shallow: the framework has shaped nearly all later vis
  theory (Tufte cites it; Munzner's marks/channels is a direct descendant) while the original text
  itself remains rarely read cover-to-cover.
- A key limitation flagged by the retrospective: the work is purely theoretical, was never
  implemented as software, and — writing in 1967 about static paper/print graphics — did not
  anticipate interactive or dynamic visualization, a gap that 50+ years of technology has not
  closed for the core taxonomy itself.
