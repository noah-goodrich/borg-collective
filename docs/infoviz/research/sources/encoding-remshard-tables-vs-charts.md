## Key Findings

- Tables are advantageous specifically when the reader's goal is to extract precise numerical
  values, specific data points, or ranks — i.e., lookup and precision tasks, not trend-spotting.
- Charts encode values as position/length/size/color and are better suited to comparisons,
  predictions, and perceiving patterns or trends across many data points at once.
- Good table design rests on three principles: aid comparisons (right-flush numeric alignment,
  tabular fonts, consistent decimal precision), reduce visual clutter (avoid heavy gridlines,
  remove redundant unit repetition), and increase readability (distinguish headers, highlight
  statistical significance, use active/descriptive titles).
- The paper's discussion section offers concrete, testable recommendations rather than vague
  aesthetic advice — a direct actionable checklist for practitioners producing scientific tables.
- This "tables = precision/lookup, charts = trend/pattern" split is corroborated (with less
  academic rigor) across general-audience data-viz blogs, suggesting the finding generalizes
  beyond the ecology/evolution publishing niche it was written for.
