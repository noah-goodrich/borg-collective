## Key Findings

- NYT's climate-visualization production process is described as sketch-first: "Step one is
  always sketching with the data and thinking through what all these layers look like" (Mira
  Rojanasakul), i.e., narrative structure and visuals are worked out together before polishing.
- Text and visuals are deliberately co-designed at the paragraph level: "We would think about
  specific paragraphs and where they would go next to specific visuals, so that the visuals would
  reinforce the paragraph" (Raymond Zhong) — a concrete practice for annotation/pacing alignment.
- A large majority of production effort goes into refinement, not initial concept: "At least 50%
  of the final work goes into sweating those details" (Rojanasakul) — a specific, checkable
  practitioner claim about where narrative-visualization labor actually goes.
- The team explicitly designs for a small number of memorable takeaways rather than comprehensive
  coverage: "We wanted it to feel like an experience you could sit through and take away three
  really solid points" (Zhong) — directly relevant to an ELI10 design bar (simplify to a few
  durable takeaways, not exhaustive detail).
- The reporters self-corrected an early draft that moved too fast for the visual medium ("My
  initial draft was we would go through the storm pretty quickly... but then when you think about
  that as a visual, that potentially is really slow"), showing that pacing mismatches between
  prose and visual are a real, named production risk — the same risk class Kosara's card names at
  the interaction-design level.
