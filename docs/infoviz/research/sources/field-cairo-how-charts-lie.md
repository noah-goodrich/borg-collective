## Key Findings

- Cairo's central claim, as distilled by the review: **"Charts don't lie, people do"** — misleadingness is located
  in intent/construction choices, not inherent to the chart form itself.
- A chart is framed as **"a visual argument... made visual,"** not a neutral picture — this reframes visualization
  as rhetoric, a direct challenge to the "charts = objective data" assumption common in practitioner training.
- Cairo explicitly positions himself against Tufte's rule-based dogmatism elsewhere in his work (per the separate
  Cairo interview found in this research pass): he "rebelled against 'the Tuftean tradition of saying, this is the
  only way to do visualization well,'" proposing instead five context-dependent qualities (truthful, functional,
  beautiful, insightful, enlightening) rather than a fixed rule set.
- The book identifies distinct mechanisms by which charts mislead even without overt malice: showing the wrong
  information, showing too little information, and poor design choices — three separable failure modes relevant
  to an ELI10 readability audit.
- Kosara's review flags a durability risk: the book leans on political examples, which "may cause the book to age
  poorly" — a currency caveat worth carrying into any curriculum built on this text.
